## 1. The statement

> Our thinking sets the limits of what we can achieve.
>
> By “thinking,” we mean our level of awareness, the sophistication of our reasoning, the breadth of our intellectual toolkit, and our ability to select, integrate, and revise frameworks in light of evidence. Together, these shape our cognitive horizon, defining how we perceive, evaluate, and act.
>
> The same applies to agents.

*D.Tadpole*

## 2. Cognitive horizon

A cognitive horizon is the scope of what a person or agent can meaningfully perceive, understand, and evaluate. It shapes which factors enter consideration, which explanations can be developed, and which actions can be assessed.

Four capacities shape this horizon: awareness, reasoning, an intellectual toolkit, and the ability to use and revise frameworks. They describe related but distinct strengths. A broad toolkit offers more approaches; sound reasoning helps assess them; awareness brings relevant information into view; framework use connects those resources to a particular situation.

A horizon has both scope and limits. Someone may understand one domain deeply while missing important factors in another. Knowledge, experience, tools, and interaction with others can extend that reach.[9][10]

## 3. Four capacities

### 3.1 Level of awareness

Awareness concerns what enters attention and consideration. It includes noticing relevant facts, recognizing relationships, and being aware of missing information or uncertainty.

For example, noticing a missed deadline is different from recognizing the dependencies, incentives, and changing requirements that may have contributed to it. Greater awareness brings more of the relevant situation into consideration.

Level of awareness appears in personal development discussions as a way to discuss differences in how people recognize their circumstances and possibilities.[1] In practice, awareness should be considered in relation to a particular situation rather than treated as one universal ranking of people.

### 3.2 Sophistication of reasoning

Reasoning connects observations to explanations, judgments, and choices. Its sophistication involves identifying assumptions, comparing explanations, tracing relationships, handling tradeoffs, and judging uncertainty.

This requires both depth and discipline. A detailed explanation can still rest on a poor assumption. Sound reasoning checks whether evidence supports a conclusion and whether another explanation fits better.

Research on cognitive sophistication considers both cognitive abilities and thinking dispositions, including open-mindedness and reasoning about belief bias, base rates, and framing.[3]

### 3.3 Breadth of the intellectual toolkit

An intellectual toolkit contains the concepts, models, frameworks, methods, and heuristics available for thinking about a problem. Breadth concerns the variety and coverage of these tools.

Different tools make different features visible. A process model may reveal a bottleneck; an incentive model may explain behavior; a probabilistic model may clarify uncertainty. Having several approaches makes it possible to examine a situation in more than one way.

Scott Page's account of a cognitive repertoire includes information, knowledge, heuristics, representations, and mental models.[4] The usefulness of such a collection depends on understanding the tools' assumptions and limits as well as knowing that they exist.

### 3.4 Selecting, integrating, and revising frameworks

A framework organizes what matters, how observations are interpreted, and which relationships are considered. Using frameworks well requires three capacities:

| Capacity | What it involves |
| --- | --- |
| Select | Choose a framework whose assumptions and scope fit the problem |
| Integrate | Connect useful perspectives while handling their agreements, differences, and tradeoffs |
| Revise | Adjust assumptions or methods when evidence calls them into question |

A framework's applicability and a person's flexibility are distinct. A framework may fit a narrow range of situations, while a person can learn when to use it and when another approach is needed.

Cognitive flexibility concerns adjusting cognitive sets or strategies as circumstances change.[5] Integrative complexity distinguishes recognizing different dimensions from recognizing connections among them.[6] These capacities help a thinker move between perspectives and connect them coherently.

Revision in light of evidence involves judging what should change and what remains supported. It calls for learning from outcomes, checking assumptions, and correcting explanations when necessary.

## 4. Thinking, action, and learning

The four capacities shape our cognitive horizon and, through it, how we perceive, evaluate, and act:

| Function | Central question |
| --- | --- |
| Perceive | What information, relationships, and possibilities can be noticed and understood? |
| Evaluate | How should evidence, alternatives, uncertainty, and tradeoffs be assessed? |
| Act | What choice should be made, and how should it be carried out? |

Perception supplies evidence for evaluation. Evaluation weighs evidence and alternatives against goals and constraints and guides action. Action produces new evidence. What can be carried out also depends on available resources and circumstances.

Learning from feedback can bring overlooked factors into view, improve reasoning, expand the intellectual toolkit, and prompt the selection, integration, or revision of frameworks. These changes can reshape the cognitive horizon and influence subsequent perception, evaluation, and action.

Learning can add knowledge and skills or develop more complex ways of organizing and interpreting experience. Adult-development and leadership literature often distinguish these as horizontal and vertical development.[7][8] Both can extend a cognitive horizon, although the four capacities need not develop at the same rate.

## 5. Humans and agents

For an agent, the four capacities concern the information it brings into consideration, how it reasons, the knowledge and methods it can draw on, and how it selects, integrates, and revises frameworks. Together, these shape which aspects of a task it can perceive, which alternatives it can evaluate, and which actions it can formulate and carry out.

An agent may have access to relevant information yet miss its significance, or possess several analytical methods yet repeatedly choose an unsuitable one. Its cognitive horizon therefore depends on both what is available and how effectively it uses it. Feedback can reshape that horizon when it changes the knowledge, methods, or assumptions the agent uses in later decisions.

## 6. An illustrative example

Consider a person or agent investigating a project that repeatedly misses delivery dates. The initial explanation is that people need to work harder. Suppose the task records show that much of the elapsed time is spent waiting for approval.

| Capacity | What it involves in this example |
| --- | --- |
| Level of awareness | Notice dependencies, approval rules, reviewer availability, and gaps in the records. |
| Sophistication of reasoning | Compare workload, changing requirements, and approval delays as explanations. Use timestamps to distinguish working time from waiting time. |
| Breadth of the intellectual toolkit | Draw on process mapping, capacity analysis, and risk analysis to examine workflow, reviewer availability, and approval requirements from different perspectives. |
| Select frameworks | Use process mapping to locate where work waits and capacity analysis to examine whether reviewer availability can meet demand. |
| Integrate frameworks | Connect mandatory centralized review with limited reviewer capacity to explain why work accumulates. |
| Revise frameworks | Revise the assumption that delivery mainly depends on individual effort when the evidence points to a shared review bottleneck. |

The broader cognitive horizon brings changes to approval rules, delegation, and reviewer capacity into consideration. These become options the person or agent can understand and assess.

Perceiving brings the queue and its consequences into view. Evaluating compares the options against time, cost, and quality requirements. Acting tests a defined change, such as delegating approval for a set of low-risk tasks.

The pilot's waiting times, delivery times, and rework rates provide feedback for reassessing the explanation and the method. Learning means using those results to improve later decisions. The practical gain is a broader set of credible ways to improve delivery, together with a way to test which ones work.

## Appendix A: Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Thinking | Awareness, reasoning, available intellectual tools, and the capacity to use and revise frameworks |
| Level of awareness | The extent to which relevant factors, relationships, gaps, and possibilities enter consideration |
| Sophistication of reasoning | Depth, refinement, and discipline in forming explanations and judgments |
| Intellectual toolkit | Concepts, models, frameworks, methods, and heuristics available for use |
| Framework | Assumptions and relationships that organize understanding or action |
| Cognitive flexibility | Adjusting cognitive sets or strategies to changing circumstances |
| Integrative complexity | Recognizing different dimensions and connections among them |
| Cognitive horizon | The scope of what can be meaningfully perceived, understood, and evaluated |
