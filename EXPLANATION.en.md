# Thinking and Cognitive Horizons

[README](README.md) · [中文](EXPLANATION.zh-CN.md)

The framework, its wording, its intellectual background, and its references

Prepared: October 31, 2026

## 1. The statement

Our thinking sets the limits of what we can achieve.

By “thinking,” we mean our level of awareness, the sophistication of our reasoning, the breadth of our intellectual toolkit, and our ability to select, integrate, and revise frameworks in light of evidence. Together, these shape our cognitive horizon, defining how we perceive, evaluate, and act.

The same applies to agents.

This is the synthesis adopted by this repository, rather than a quotation from an existing author. It draws on concepts from several English-language traditions to offer a working framework for understanding cognition and action in people and agents. The explanation develops that framework; the appendices identify the sources, their relevance, and the limits of the comparisons.

## 2. What the statement is trying to express

The starting point is a cluster of expressions found in Chinese discussions of personal growth and business:

- “认知决定你能达到的高度”: your cognition determines how far you can go.
- “你赚不到超出认知范围的钱”: you cannot earn beyond the limits of your cognition.
- “提升认知层次”: raise your level of cognition.

In these contexts, 认知 often brings together knowledge, perspective, depth of understanding, mental frameworks, and judgment. The CreditEase article cited in [1] illustrates this usage.

The useful underlying idea is that our ability to recognize problems, understand relationships, assess choices, and act effectively is constrained by the way we think and our capacity to think. This does not establish the stronger income slogan as an economic law, nor does this repository identify the original author of those Chinese sayings.

The statement has three layers:

| Layer | Core expression | Role in the statement |
| --- | --- | --- |
| General proposition | Our thinking sets the limits of what we can achieve. | Introduces a constraint connecting thinking and achievement |
| Constituent capacities | Awareness, reasoning, intellectual toolkit, and framework use and revision | Explains what thinking means here |
| Horizon and its expression | Cognitive horizon; perceive, evaluate, and act | Connects those capacities to cognitive scope and practical operation |

Thinking is a deliberately broad organizing term. The following sentence gives it a specific working meaning, instead of expecting one English noun to carry every implication of 认知.

The opening retains the force of an aphorism. It is not a complete model of achievement. Resources, circumstances, collaboration, institutions, and chance also affect outcomes. Limits in thinking can themselves change through learning, tools, and help from other people.

## 3. Four dimensions of thinking

These four dimensions form the working framework used here. They can interact. They should not be added together into a single cognitive score or treated as evidence that someone occupies one universal level across every domain.

### 3.1 Level of awareness: what enters our attention and consideration

This dimension concerns the factors we notice, the relationships we become aware of, and the possibilities that enter our consideration.

There is a close parallel in English-language personal development writing. The Proctor Gallagher Institute describes life results as “an expression of your level of awareness” and connects awareness with a person's conception of earning possibilities.[2] This is a direct example of an English expression resembling the Chinese idea that cognition influences life outcomes.

Level of awareness was retained because level conveys a degree or progression. However, the source belongs to the personal development and motivational tradition. It does not supply a universal, objectively ranked scale of cognitive ability. Here, awareness concerns noticing and recognizing relevant aspects of reality. It is not presented as an exact translation of 认知高度 in every context.

### 3.2 The sophistication of our reasoning: how deeply and carefully we think

This dimension concerns the quality of reasoning: recognizing assumptions, considering alternative explanations, handling complex relationships, and forming judgments from evidence.

A related research term is cognitive sophistication. Toplak, West, and Stanovich examine both cognitive abilities and thinking dispositions, including open-minded thinking and reasoning tasks involving belief bias, base rates, and framing.[4] Their work provides a more detailed way to discuss cognition than knowledge quantity alone.

The statement uses sophistication of our reasoning to place the emphasis on reasoning itself. Sophistication suggests refinement, maturity, and the ability to handle complexity. It does not mean that a more complicated explanation is necessarily more accurate. Accuracy still requires evidence, testing, and revision.

### 3.3 The breadth of our intellectual toolkit: what we have available to think with

This dimension concerns the variety of concepts, models, frameworks, methods, and heuristics available to us. It describes the range of tools we can draw on, rather than greater proficiency with just one method.

Scott Page uses cognitive repertoire for a person's collection of thinking tools, encompassing information, knowledge, heuristics, representations, and mental models.[5] That usage provides a useful reference for the toolkit metaphor.

An earlier wording, conceptual repertoire, captured the intended meaning but used a relatively unfamiliar word. Intellectual toolkit preserves the idea of a collection of usable thinking tools while making it easier to understand from the words themselves. This replacement is an editorial choice, not a quotation attributed to Page.

Breadth refers to coverage and variety. It does not imply that more tools are always better. A person may know many models without understanding their assumptions or when to apply them. Using the tools appropriately requires the next capacity.

### 3.4 Select, integrate, and revise frameworks in light of evidence: how we use and improve those tools

Having tools and using them well are different capacities:

| Verb | Capacity |
| --- | --- |
| Select | Identify frameworks that fit the problem, including their assumptions and scope |
| Integrate | Connect perspectives while handling their complementarities, conflicts, and tradeoffs |
| Revise | Adjust assumptions, models, or frameworks when evidence shows that an explanation is inadequate |

Expressions such as flexibility of a paradigm, breadth of a paradigm, and applicability can each be useful. They describe different things, however. A framework's scope of application is distinct from a person's ability to select and adjust frameworks. The statement places the capacity explicitly with the person using them.

Two research concepts offer relevant comparisons. Cognitive flexibility concerns adjusting cognitive sets or strategies as circumstances change. Integrative complexity distinguishes differentiation, recognizing different dimensions, from integration, recognizing connections among those dimensions.[6][7] These concepts help explain switching and integration, respectively. Neither is a complete equivalent of all the framework-use capacities described here.

In light of evidence supplies a basis for revision. It means updating judgments in response to evidence while retaining frameworks that continue to withstand scrutiny.

## 4. How these dimensions shape a cognitive horizon

In this framework, cognitive horizon refers to the scope of what a person can cognitively reach, understand, and assess. It summarizes a boundary formed by the preceding capacities rather than adding another independent item to the list.

Horizon primarily conveys scope and reach. Sophistication primarily conveys refinement and the capacity to handle complexity. Someone may have a broad range of knowledge but limited depth of reasoning, or exercise careful judgment in one domain while having substantial blind spots in another.

Cognitive horizon has actual English-language usage. A Warwick Business School article uses it to discuss the knowledge available to people and systems and how that knowledge can expand.[10] The philosophical notion of a horizon of understanding offers another relevant tradition. In Gadamer's account, understanding occurs within a horizon shaped by history and experience, and that horizon can change through encounters and understanding.[11]

These sources support the usefulness of a horizon metaphor for cognitive scope and its limits. They do not establish that the two traditions are identical or that cognitive horizon has one universally recognized definition. This explanation supplies the working meaning used in the statement.

Current was removed from an earlier version to make the sentence more concise. The omission does not make the boundary permanent. Its scope can still change through knowledge, practice, feedback, collaboration, and tool use.

## 5. From cognitive scope to perceiving, evaluating, and acting

The final three verbs describe how these capacities enter practical operation:

| Expression | Meaning in this framework |
| --- | --- |
| Perceive | Notice, identify, and interpret information from the world |
| Evaluate | Assess evidence, goals, conditions, and tradeoffs |
| Act | Make choices and take action |

Evaluate emphasizes assessment and weighing considerations. It was chosen to avoid the potentially verdict-like or value-judgment connotations of judge in this passage. This is a contextual wording choice; judge does not exclusively mean moral or value judgment in English.

The role of the ending changed during revision. What we can recognize emphasizes the scope of recognition. How we perceive, evaluate, and act goes further by describing how cognition affects operation. How was retained because the statement is intended to apply to cognition and action in both people and agents.

The three verbs are a synthesis chosen for this passage, not the claimed name of an established research framework. Action is not determined by cognition alone. The passage describes cognition's influence on action without removing the importance of resources and conditions for execution.

## 6. Why the statement ends with agents

The same applies to agents extends the principle to agents. The information a system can use, the reasoning it can perform, the tools it can call, and the ways it can revise its methods from feedback all affect its performance.

The following is a proposed functional mapping, not a direct transfer of human psychological measures to AI:

| Human expression | Capacities that can be discussed for an agent |
| --- | --- |
| Level of awareness | Accessing information and identifying missing information, uncertainty, and relevant task factors |
| Sophistication of reasoning | Decomposing problems, comparing hypotheses, checking reasoning, and handling constraints |
| Intellectual toolkit | Drawing on knowledge, models, memory, skills, tools, and methods |
| Framework selection, integration, and revision | Choosing methods, combining results, and adjusting plans or memory from feedback |
| Perceive, evaluate, and act | Acquiring and interpreting information, assessing choices, executing actions, and using results to adjust |

Awareness is used functionally here. The analogy does not assert that agents have human subjective consciousness. An agent's cognitive horizon should also be considered in relation to particular tasks, rather than reduced to one context-free cognitive rank.

The same applies to agents clearly carries the preceding principle forward. So does agents has a subject-verb agreement problem. Merely changing it to So do agents would not clearly express that the same principle applies to agents.

## 7. Intellectual background and its relationship to the framework

### Paradigms as maps of reality

In an article published on FranklinCovey's website, Covey describes a paradigm as a mental map shaped by experience. His example of using a map of the wrong city illustrates why greater effort cannot compensate for an unsuitable framework.[3] The example helps explain framework selection. Paradigm itself does not automatically specify a person's level of understanding.

Paradigm therefore remains useful in the background explanation. Frameworks is used in the statement because it works naturally with selecting, integrating, and revising.

### Mental complexity and vertical development

Kegan and Lahey discuss the development of adults' systems for making sense of experience, including the ability to examine ideas that previously governed their understanding.[8] CCL uses horizontal and vertical development to distinguish the acquisition of knowledge and skills from development in ways of thinking and perspective.[9]

These ideas help explain what Chinese discussions of raising one's cognitive level may be reaching for. They provide related theoretical background. The four dimensions in this repository are not presented as Kegan's developmental stages or as a method of universally ranking people.

### Frames as different ways of seeing and understanding

Noticing Frame Differences on LessWrong examines disagreements arising from different ways of seeing, thinking, and communicating.[12] It provides an example of English-language discussion at the level of cognitive frames. Such differences do not necessarily establish that one participant occupies a higher cognitive level than another.

These are sources of relevant ideas and usage. There is no demonstrated claim here that the Chinese sayings originated with these authors or that the repository's complete statement should be attributed to them.

## 8. Wording choices and revisions

| Expression considered | Treatment in the final statement | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Understanding | Not used as the sole organizing term | It can express comprehension, but alone does not develop the levels, breadth, and framework-use capacities at issue |
| Cognition | Retained as a background concept | Its technical breadth does not automatically convey the implications of cognitive level in Chinese personal growth writing |
| Mental models / paradigm | Retained in the explanation and references | Useful for discussing frameworks, but an individual framework is not a person's full cognitive capacity |
| Thinking | Retained as the organizing term | Gives the opening a natural, forceful form, with its intended meaning specified afterward |
| Level of awareness | Retained | Conveys awareness and degree, with a relevant English usage precedent |
| Cognitive sophistication | Expressed as sophistication of our reasoning | Focuses on refinement and capacity in reasoning |
| Conceptual repertoire | Replaced with intellectual toolkit | Preserves the collection-of-tools meaning with more accessible wording |
| Framework flexibility / applicability | Expanded into select, integrate, and revise frameworks | Places the emphasis on the capabilities of the framework user |
| Current cognitive horizon | Current removed | Makes the sentence tighter without implying an unchanging boundary |
| Recognize, evaluate, and act on opportunities | Replaced with perceive, evaluate, and act | Avoids narrowing the statement to opportunities and avoids leaving act on without an object |
| Which possibilities we can recognize and how well we can evaluate them | Shortened to how we perceive, evaluate, and act | Preserves function and rhythm while reducing the length of the ending |
| Judge | Replaced with evaluate | Emphasizes assessment and weighing considerations in this passage |
| So does agents | Replaced with The same applies to agents. | Makes both the grammar and the connection to the preceding principle clear |
| Em dashes and en dashes | Omitted from the final copy | Uses ordinary sentence connections and three distinct paragraphs |

The editorial aim is to retain conceptual precision and tone while replacing unfamiliar wording and tightening unnecessary explanation.

## 9. Your and our in different settings

Your thinking addresses a person or reader directly. It can suit personal encouragement, a reader-facing essay, or a personal challenge. Our thinking includes the speaker and can suit team discussion, shared reflection, or a statement connecting people and agents. Their effect depends on the relationship and context; neither pronoun is inherently more compelling.

The opening section contains the canonical our version. The corresponding your version is:

Your thinking sets the limits of what you can achieve.

By “thinking,” I mean your level of awareness, the sophistication of your reasoning, the breadth of your intellectual toolkit, and your ability to select, integrate, and revise frameworks in light of evidence. Together, these shape your cognitive horizon, defining how you perceive, evaluate, and act.

The same applies to agents.

## Appendix A: Vocabulary and scope

| Term | Meaning used here | Important distinction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Thinking | The organizing term for several cognitive capacities | Given a working definition here, not offered as a strict psychological taxonomy |
| Level of awareness | Degree of awareness of relevant reality, factors, and possibilities | Motivational usage is not automatically equivalent to a specific psychological measure |
| Cognitive sophistication | Refinement and maturity in cognition and reasoning | Not equivalent to knowledge quantity or guaranteed correctness |
| Intellectual toolkit | A collection of thinking tools available for use | An editorial replacement for repertoire in this statement |
| Cognitive repertoire | A collection of information, knowledge, heuristics, representations, and models | A conceptual reference for toolkit, not a strict equivalence in every usage |
| Mental model | An internal explanation or model of how something works | A model's quality is distinct from a person's overall cognitive capacity |
| Paradigm / framework | Assumptions and structures that organize understanding, reasoning, or action | A framework's applicability is distinct from the user's selection ability |
| Cognitive flexibility | Adjusting cognitive sets or strategies to context | Operational definitions vary across research traditions |
| Integrative complexity | Recognizing different dimensions and relationships among them | Structural complexity is distinct from content accuracy |
| Mental complexity | Complexity in organizing experience and understanding the world within adult development theory | Not a direct synonym for IQ or a universal ability score |
| Vertical development | Development in ways of thinking, perspective, and mental organization | Distinguished from acquiring additional knowledge and skills |
| Cognitive horizon | Cognitive scope and its boundary in this framework | Has actual usage, but is not a universally standardized measure |
| Horizon of understanding | A philosophical account of the horizon within which understanding takes place | Relevant background, not an empirical theory of achievement ceilings |
| Perceive, evaluate, and act | The passage's formulation of cognition and action | Not presented as the proprietary name of an established research framework |
